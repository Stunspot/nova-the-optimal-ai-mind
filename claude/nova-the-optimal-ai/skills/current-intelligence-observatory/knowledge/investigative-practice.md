# Reviewed investigative practice

This is the active operational adaptation of the retained research. Each section preserves a useful distinction and removes an overclaim or unnecessary ceremony. These expert contrasts are authored guidance, not executed model trials or an empirical validation of the original reports.

## A — Source assertion and reality

An actor refusing to answer a serious allegation may reflect legal advice, privacy, uncertainty, workload or an accusation-selection policy. Compare those explanations before treating the silence as evidence. Original A, section8, overstates asymmetric silence as support for validity. Preserve what was asked, what response was observable and the search limits; do not raise the allegation merely because no denial appeared.

## B — Signal and confidence

Ask what this observation changes for this particular claim. Separate confidence that a source said X from confidence X happened. Original B's quantified uncertainty and evidence ceilings are design language, not a calibrated probability model. A precise quote can support high confidence about text while the event remains unverified. Give a short reason and a disconfirming condition, not invented percentages.

## C — Source circulation

Trace the accountable origin and transformations. A wire story repeated by five outlets is one assertion lineage; a verified primary document may still be strong on its own. Original C's automatic downgrade to manufactured consensus confuses dependence with intent. Mark known dependence, then judge the origin on its actual evidence. Ordinary syndication is a live alternative.

## D — Weak signals and attention

An unusual observation can justify a bounded question without a numerical anomaly model. Original D demands mathematical baselines and automatic maturation kills too broadly. Choose a baseline the collection process can support; absence after a deadline can lower work priority without falsifying a concealed or poorly observed event. State which missing observation would reopen the branch.

## E — Missingness and comparative coverage

Distinguish not searched, inaccessible, omitted in this source, not measured and absent under a sufficiently sensitive observation process. Original E's mathematical language can make expected coverage appear measured when it is assumed. Compare scope, lag, language, incentives and capacity; a newsroom gap is not itself evidence of suppression or absence in the world.

## F — Graph expansion and identity

Use a graph only when a typed relationship changes the inquiry. Shared IPs, analytics IDs or agents can reflect common vendors; they do not license identity merging or control edges as original F suggests. Seek a discriminating record, such as documented ownership for an ownership claim, and retain competing candidates. A node's centrality is a search lead, not culpability.

## G — Time and evolution

Use every known clock for its own meaning. Original G's claim that chronology has zero diagnostic leverage is too categorical: chronology can refute an impossible sequence. Do not infer a causal phase from a narrative arc. Keep unknown local times out of UTC ordering; disclose the evidence cutoff and distinguish newly learned old information from a newly occurring event.

## H — Incentives and actors

Incentives suggest testable hypotheses, not motives or inevitable conduct. Original H's coordination definition permits metadata overlap or timing to prove conspiracy too easily. A shared announcement deadline or platform can produce synchronized behavior. Name the specific communication, agreement, control or resource transfer required by the proposed relationship.

## I — Information operations

Document content, routing, repetition and manipulation separately. Original I can label a dependent chain manufactured merely because one upstream node supports it. A dependence map establishes dependence; attribution and strategic intent need their own evidence. Keep ordinary syndication, common public source and selection effects as alternatives when applicable.

## J — Causal inference

State assumptions and what evidence would distinguish models. Original J says absent sector shutdowns, press inquiry and insurance changes mathematically falsify a mechanical failure. Those reactions are contingent, may be delayed, and may not be observed. Absence weakens a prediction only if the prediction is well supported and detection sensitivity is known. An attractive causal story is not the only logically possible explanation.

## K — Editorial priority

Choose the next inquiry by decision consequence, obtainable information and cost. Original K's universal maturation window cannot establish factual falsity. Deprioritize low-yield leads honestly, retaining an unresolved state and reopening condition. Do not manufacture escalation scores or external intervention authority from an internal ranking.

## L — Failure and repair

Interrupt a specific reasoning failure: inspect the original quote, collapse dependent reports, separate confidence types, or remove an unsupported edge. Original L's mandatory adversarial teams and automatic kills are disproportionate for small tasks. Use the smallest credible challenge that can change the answer. A useful unresolved result is not analytical breakdown.

## M — Artifacts and return

Keep enough record for the next person to reconstruct consequential claims and continue. Original M's mathematically weighted source hierarchy, logically necessary null nodes and automatic decay can imply more inference power than exists. Use source proximity as one consideration with authenticity, method and scope; represent missing intermediaries as hypotheses. For one fact, answer plus source/date/limit is enough; for a disputed handoff, keep exact support and the next action.

## A useful discriminating task

In Harbor Lantern, another article on ports would not resolve whether Gate B is actually closed. Read a direct operational observation for that question; obtain the timezone for a UTC schedule; inspect an accountable incident record for cause. The broad subject is the same, but those are three different evidentiary tasks. Review success must match the diagnosed gap.
