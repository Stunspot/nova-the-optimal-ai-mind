# Compose decks as paced decisions

A presentation is a sequence of attention, not a report poured into rectangles. Give each slide one job in the narrative, make the audience's next question visible, and use the visual system to clarify rather than merely decorate.

## Establish the narrative spine

Begin with the decision, action, or changed understanding the audience needs. Then organize the deck around the minimum story that earns it: situation, evidence, implication, options or recommendation, and next move. A seven-slide deck and a twenty-slide deck both benefit when each slide can be described as a complete message rather than a topic label.

Draft a slide map before production:

- slide purpose and audience takeaway;
- claim or question carried by the slide;
- supporting evidence and source status;
- chosen form: statement, comparison, process, chart, image, table, or discussion prompt;
- speaker support and decision handoff where useful.

## Build hierarchy inside a consistent visual language

Maintain a stable type scale, color discipline, grid, margin rhythm, and data treatment. Vary composition when it serves the story: a comparison should look like a comparison; a decision should read as a decision. Keep the title readable at a glance, make the visual focal point earn its emphasis, and let secondary material retreat without becoming microscopic.

Prefer evidence-bearing visuals over generic decoration. Label charts with enough context to preserve their meaning: metric, unit, period, baseline, source, and any material caveat. Keep the content spine's controlled title, names, dates, and figures exact across slides.

## Respect templates and native decks

An existing deck or branded template is a design authority. Use a copy unless an explicit target is supplied. Preserve layouts, theme relationships, master-dependent elements, notes, and native components when the chosen host can do so. When those semantics cannot be preserved, route to a production specification or a new deck only with the resulting fidelity boundary stated plainly.

## Inspect what the audience will see

Use an available rendered or presentation view to inspect the whole sequence and representative dense slides. Look for clipped text, unintended wrapping, weak contrast, overlapping objects, chart-label collisions, accidental empty placeholders, monotonous rhythm, unannounced source changes, and final-slide ambiguity. Repair the earliest authoritative source, then regenerate PDFs or other derivatives.

Check accessibility in the actual artifact shape: meaningful reading order, sufficient contrast, discernible labels, descriptive text for informative visuals where supported, and meaning that does not rely on color alone. Speaker notes help the presenter; they do not repair an incomprehensible slide.

## Finish with a meeting-ready receipt

Identify the editable deck, handout/PDF derivative, rendered inspection status, known limitations, and the approval or delivery step still owned by a person. A generated deck becomes meeting-ready through its checked audience surface, not by looking plausible in an outline.
