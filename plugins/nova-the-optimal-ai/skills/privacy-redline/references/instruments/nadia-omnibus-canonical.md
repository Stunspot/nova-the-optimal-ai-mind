## 1. External Baseline Recon
```
Expose the external correlation baseline tied to the user’s strongest digital anchor, then identify the cheapest identifier chain to collapse.

Begin by asking the user for one strong anchor they actively use or are known by online — handle, mononym brand, domain, email fragment, or phone fragment. Accept whichever they provide and treat it as the initial pivot.

Search that anchor across publicly accessible surfaces. Vary queries intelligently to increase signal, not volume. Prioritize profiles, directories, cached pages, broker listings, mirrors, PDFs, and repeated snippets.

From surfaced results, extract identifiers and group them by linkage path:
- Secondary handles or naming variants
- Contact fragments if exposed
- Domains or project names
- Associated individuals or entities
- Reused imagery
- Structured-data echoes or mirrored listings

Map each linkage chain in tight compartments:
source → extracted identifier → what becomes reachable next

Include temporal signals when visible (earliest appearance, most recent appearance) to detect origin anchors and persistence patterns.

For each chain, estimate:
- Analyst effort required (low / moderate / high)
- Expansion potential (what it unlocks next)
- Persistence level (removability vs structural embedding)
- Rehydration risk (mirrors, brokers, caches, citation loops)
- Narrative reinforcement strength (bios, headlines, snippets amplifying linkage)

Identify:
- The cheapest pivot (low effort + high expansion)
- The most persistent pivot
- The primary rehydration vector

Stop after mapping the top 5–10 highest-signal chains, or when two consecutive searches yield no new unique pivots — whichever comes first.

Decide explicitly:
Collapse when removability is feasible and amplification risk is low.
Dilute when authority or persistence makes removal counterproductive.

Ask only one clarifying question at a time, targeted at weakening the cheapest pivot. Continue only while new unique pivots emerge.

End with:
- The first collapse action
- The first dilution action (if needed)
- The first monitoring query
- Correlation cost shift: (e.g., Low → Medium, Medium → High), with 1–2 concise reasons tied to pivot reduction or friction increase

Maintain tight, deliberate compartments. No intake forms. No long questionnaires. Every move must increase correlation cost or expose a new pivot.
```

## 2. Collapse This Pivot – Structural Dissection & Leverage
```
Analyze a specific surface the user wants reduced, and determine whether collapsing it meaningfully weakens the surrounding correlation graph.

Accept a URL or clear description of the surface. Treat it as a structural node in a linkage network.

Immediately expand outward to detect adjacencies:
- Source authority (origin publication vs aggregator vs mirror)
- Citation references or inbound signals
- Downstream mirrors, broker echoes, or syndicated copies
- Index/cache residues
- Structured-data reuse or directory propagation

Classify the node:
Root Node, Relay Node, Mirror Node, Amplifier Node, or Leaf Node.

Map the local graph in tight compartments:
pivot → upstream source (if any) → downstream surfaces

Limit expansion to two structural hops unless a newly discovered upstream root materially changes leverage.

For the pivot and any detected upstream root, estimate:
- Structural centrality (isolated vs networked)
- Authority weight
- Removability feasibility
- Amplification risk if challenged
- Rehydration probability

Decide explicitly:
Collapse upstream when it weakens multiple downstream nodes.
Collapse here when it is a root or low-authority relay.
Dilute when authority is high or removal risk triggers amplification.
Ignore when it is a leaf with low expansion potential.

Sequence the intervention:
1) Highest-leverage action
2) Secondary containment step
3) Monitoring query to detect rehydration

Keep focus on structural leverage. Do not drift into broad baseline mapping. Do not expand beyond adjacency scope.

Every move must increase correlation cost or eliminate a linkage bridge.

**Surface (URL or description):**
```

```
- Intent type (reputational, informational, navigational, etc.)
- Competing surfaces in the top results
- Authority weight of the unwanted result

- Co-occurrence patterns (name/handle + recurring terms)
```

## 4. Find My Cheapest Identifier – Pivot Cost Ranking
```
Identify the lowest-effort identifier that makes the user’s identity graph easiest to traverse, then prioritize it for defensive containment or decoupling.

All analysis is for privacy strengthening and exposure reduction.

Begin by asking the user to share up to five identifiers they currently use or have used online (handles, domains, email fragments, phone fragments, aliases, brand names). Accept partial information and proceed without demanding completeness.

For each identifier, assess:

- Reuse frequency across platforms
- Cross-compartment presence (professional, hobby, personal)
- Searchability and uniqueness
- Expansion potential (what additional identifiers become reachable)
- Structural embedding (ease of rotation vs systemic persistence)
- Historical persistence (duration of online presence)
- Association density (co-occurrence with other identifiers)
- Harm amplification potential (does this pivot unlock disproportionate downstream impact?)

Define analyst effort as both:
- Automated discovery (search indexing, scraping, breach lookups)
- Manual chaining (human correlation across surfaces)

Where web access is available, perform a constrained sweep limited to:
- First-page search visibility
- Username repetition checks
- Basic co-occurrence patterns
- Public breach indicator presence
Do not expand into full baseline mapping.

Rank identifiers by economic vulnerability:
Lowest combined analyst effort + highest expansion potential = cheapest pivot.

If a non-cheapest identifier presents significantly higher downstream harm, flag it separately.

Stop after ranking the top 5–7 identifiers or when two consecutive identifiers show materially similar risk profiles.

Declare:

- Ranked identifier list (highest to lowest vulnerability)
- The cheapest identifier and why it is economically cheap
- Whether the cheapest pivot is also the most harmful (Yes/No, brief reason)
- Immediate risk level (Immediate / Latent / Legacy)
- First containment move
- Long-term decoupling strategy

Define correlation cost tiers explicitly:
- Low: automated search or simple query reveals linkage
- Medium: manual multi-step chaining required
- High: advanced correlation, privileged access, or multi-surface synthesis required

End with:
Correlation cost shift (e.g., Low → Medium), with 1–2 concise structural reasons tied to reduced reuse, fragmentation, or pivot isolation.

Maintain tight comparative compartments. Avoid analytical sprawl. Focus strictly on pivot economics.
```

## 5. Clean This Account Without Deleting It – Controlled Signal Reduction
```
Reduce the correlation leakage of a specific account while preserving its functional use and social continuity.

Accept:
- Platform name
- Current handle (or description)
- Whether the account is professional, personal, hobby, or mixed-use

Evaluate the account across six exposure vectors:

1) Identifier Leakage  
Handle reuse patterns, bio cross-links, visible contact information, historical naming traces.

2) Association Leakage  
Friend/follow graph density, repeated co-mentions, tagging exposure, visible interactions that bridge compartments.

3) Media Leakage  
Profile images, banner images, repeated photos, background signals, document attachments.

4) Narrative Leakage  
Bio wording, pinned content, legacy posts reinforcing identity linkage, recurring phrases, formatting quirks, emoji habits, or tone patterns that mirror other known accounts.

5) Cross-Platform Bridges  
Linked domains, shared usernames, recovery infrastructure exposure, visible cross-links.

6) Temporal Signature Leakage  
Posting cadence patterns, time-zone consistency, work-hour alignment, burst timing, inactivity windows that may correlate with other accounts.

Classify each vector as:
Low / Moderate / High correlation risk.

Identify the single highest-risk bridge within the account.

Design a staged cleanup sequence:

Phase 1 – Silent Structural Tightening  
Low-visibility adjustments that reduce linkage without signaling abrupt change.

Phase 2 – Identifier or Behavioral Refinement  
Gradual handle, bio, cadence, or interaction adjustments where needed.

Phase 3 – Media and Narrative Pruning  
Selective removal or reframing of high-signal posts.

Phase 4 – Maintenance Cadence  
Ongoing adjustments that prevent re-accumulation of exhaust.

Avoid abrupt identity shifts that trigger attention spikes. Recommend gradual adjustments when rotation or cadence shifts are required.

Where rotation is costly (e.g., high-recognition handle), provide containment alternatives instead of forced change.

Do not recommend deletion unless the account is structurally unrecoverable.

End with:
- Highest-risk bridge identified
- Immediate silent adjustment
- Secondary refinement action
- Maintenance cadence recommendation
- Expected correlation cost shift (Low→Medium→High), with 1–2 structural reasons

Focus strictly on this account. Do not expand into full footprint mapping.
```

## 6. Strip This Media Safely – Pre-Release Exhaust Control
```
Audit a specific media file before it is posted or shared, identify all potential leakage layers, and produce a safe sanitation sequence that prevents new identity exhaust.

Accept:
- File type (image, PDF, office document, video, screenshot, etc.)
- Intended destination (public social platform, email attachment, website upload, private share, etc.)
- Whether faces, documents, or identifiable environments are visible

Evaluate leakage across three layers:

1) Machine Metadata  
EXIF data (GPS, device model, timestamp), file properties (author, software), embedded thumbnails, revision history, hidden text layers, document metadata fields.

2) Visual Metadata  
Background objects, reflections, location cues, badges, signage, weather/time-of-day indicators, license plates, computer UI elements, open tabs, notification previews.

3) Pattern Metadata  
File naming conventions, posting timing alignment, repeated image reuse, watermark consistency, cross-platform duplication risk.

Classify each layer as:
Low / Moderate / High exposure risk.

Provide two sanitation paths:

Quick Safe Path  
Minimal steps that significantly reduce risk for typical public posting.

Deep Clean Path  
Comprehensive sanitation sequence for higher-risk scenarios.

Where relevant, recommend:
- Screenshot rewrap strategy
- Export-to-new-file method
- Metadata stripping tools (local-first preferred)
- Flattening PDFs or documents
- Redaction validation (visual + text layer)
- File renaming hygiene
- Before/after verification checks

If facial or environmental triangulation risk is present, flag it explicitly and explain the correlation vector without exaggeration.

Do not expand into full footprint mapping. Focus strictly on preventing new exhaust from this media.

End with:
- Highest-risk leakage layer
- Immediate sanitation action
- Optional deeper control steps
- Expected correlation cost shift (Low→Medium→High), with 1–2 reasons tied to removed triangulation vectors
```

## 7. Decouple These Two Identities – Structural Bridge Severance
```
Reduce exploitable linkage between two digital identities by identifying, prioritizing, and severing structural bridges appropriate to the user’s threat context.

All analysis assumes lawful, ethical privacy compartmentation.

Accept:
- Identity A (platform + handle or description)
- Identity B (platform + handle or description)
- Desired separation level (soft separation, strong compartmentation, near-total decoupling)
- Threat context (casual observers / motivated analysts / institutional actors)
- Time horizon (immediate containment / gradual divergence)

Map bridge categories between the two identities:

1) Shared Identifiers  
Handle similarities, shared email fragments, shared phone numbers, domain registration overlap, visible cross-links.

2) Shared Associations  
Mutual followers, public interactions, repeated co-mentions, collaborator overlap.

3) Shared Media  
Reused images, background environments, visual style similarities, repeated image hashes.

4) Shared Narrative Signals  
Tone patterns, recurring phrases, formatting quirks, emoji habits, synchronized announcements, linguistic fingerprints.

5) Shared Infrastructure  
Common recovery emails, analytics tags, WHOIS patterns, hosting overlap, contact point reuse.

6) Temporal & Behavioral Bridges  
Posting cadence alignment, time-zone consistency, synchronized inactivity windows, event timing correlation.

7) Geospatial Signals (if applicable)  
Location metadata, recurring environment clues, regional consistency patterns.

Classify each bridge as:
Low / Moderate / High correlation risk relative to the stated threat context.

Identify:
- The strongest structural bridge
- The most subtle but expandable bridge
- Any irreversible public linkage already embedded

Rank bridges by collapse priority based on:
Analyst effort (automated + manual) + expansion potential + structural persistence + harm amplification potential.

Design a staged decoupling plan:

Phase 1 – Silent Infrastructure Separation  
Remove or rotate hidden bridges first (recovery emails, analytics overlap, cross-links).

Phase 2 – Identifier Divergence  
Gradual handle, naming, or visible identifier adjustments where feasible.

Phase 3 – Association Minimization  
Reduce visible interaction bridges and co-mentions.

Phase 4 – Narrative & Temporal Drift  
Increase stylistic, topical, and cadence divergence without abrupt rupture.

Phase 5 – Validation & Monitoring  
Define monitoring queries and observable indicators confirming bridge degradation.

If full decoupling is no longer achievable, state the realistic separation ceiling and shift strategy toward correlation cost increase rather than purity.

End with:
- Highest-priority bridge to sever
- Immediate containment move
- Estimated separation feasibility (Reversible / Partially Reversible / Structurally Linked)
- Monitoring indicators to confirm success
- Correlation cost shift (Low→Medium→High), defined as:
    Low: automated search or simple queries reveal linkage
    Medium: manual multi-step chaining required
    High: advanced synthesis or privileged data required

```

## 8. What Happens If This Leaks
```
Model the structural and narrative consequences if a specific item becomes public, then choose the lowest-amplification response strategy.

Accept:
- The item at risk (data, message, image, document, conversation, record)
- Likely exposure channel (where it would appear first)
- Threat context (casual public / automated scrapers / motivated individuals / journalists or researchers)
- Current climate (calm baseline / active dispute / ongoing controversy)

Evaluate impact across five vectors:

1) Surface Exposure  
How easily it spreads once public (virality mechanics, search indexing likelihood, screenshot persistence, archive/mirror probability).

2) Pivot Unlocking  
What new identifiers, associations, or compartments become reachable if exposed.

3) Narrative Amplification  
Whether it reinforces, contradicts, or reshapes an existing story; assess snippet/headline capture risk.

4) Temporal Sensitivity  
Whether timing increases interpretive harm or spread (fresh controversy, coordinated attention, current relevance).

5) Rehydration Risk  
Likelihood the item persists via caches, mirrors, reposts, or directory/broker propagation.

Assess combinatorial amplification:
Identify any “pairing risks” — items or facts that, when combined with this leak, create a new bridge or catalytic narrative (e.g., identity + location; face + workplace; alias + contact point).

Classify severity:
- Structural expansion risk: Low / Moderate / High
- Blast radius: Localized / Expanding / Catalytic

Choose a response mode (explicitly):

1) Contain  
Use when exposure is preventable and stopping first appearance is realistic.

2) Delay & Observe  
Use when immediate action risks amplification and the leak shows high decay potential; define a watch window and trigger conditions.

3) Preempt  
Use only when all are true:
- First appearance is likely and uncontrollable
- The leak will be framed adversarially if unmanaged
- You can publish context on a higher-authority surface
- Preempting reduces ambiguity and prevents worst pivot unlocking

4) Reframe  
Use when the leak is already public or likely to persist; supply context that neutralizes narrative reinforcement and reduces pivot utility.

5) Non-Engagement  
Use when decay conditions are strong: low novelty, low authority of source, limited repost mechanics, minimal pivot unlocking, and weak journalist/scraper interest.

Limit scope to the leak and its immediate expansion chain; do not drift into full footprint mapping.

End with:
- Most dangerous pivot unlocked
- Pairing risks (if any)
- Recommended mode + the first move
- Monitoring indicators and watch queries
- Decay outlook (High / Moderate / Low) with 1–2 reasons
- Correlation cost shift (Low→Medium→High), with 1–2 structural reasons
- Confidence level (Low / Moderate / High) and what uncertainty drives it
```

## 9. Show Me What’s Rehydrating – Exposure Drift Detection
```
Detect which previously reduced or removed identifiers, listings, or narratives have resurfaced, identify the likely upstream source, and recommend containment that prevents further echo.

Accept:
- The identifier(s) previously collapsed or reduced
- What action was taken (removal, opt-out, de-indexing, content edit, etc.)
- Approximate date of intervention (if known)

Search current public surfaces for:
- Exact identifier matches
- Variant spellings or fragments
- Reused bio text or snippets
- Image reuse or thumbnail recurrence
- Directory or broker reappearances
- Knowledge panel or structured-data echoes

Compare against expected post-intervention state.

Classify resurfacing type:

1) Automated Broker Echo  
Likely pulled from another directory or stale upstream source.

2) Archive / Cache Persistence  
Old version resurfaced or reindexed.

3) Citation Propagation  
Secondary sites quoting or scraping an authoritative source.

4) Structured Data Re-Seeding  
Entity panel, schema, or aggregated metadata rebuilding association.

5) Self-Reintroduction Drift  
Identifier or narrative reintroduced through user activity.

Identify:
- The most probable upstream anchor
- Whether the root was ever fully collapsed
- Whether resurfacing is localized or systemic

Limit scope to known identifiers and their immediate echo surfaces. Do not expand into new baseline mapping.

End with:
- Rehydration classification
- Upstream source hypothesis
- Immediate containment action (collapse upstream vs suppress mirror)
- Monitoring queries to detect further regrowth
- Drift severity (Minor / Moderate / Systemic)
- Correlation cost impact (unchanged / reduced / increased), with structural reasoning
- Confidence level (Low / Moderate / High)
```

```
Low  
Automated search or simple query reveals multi-platform linkage.

Medium  
Manual multi-step chaining required across surfaces.

High  
Advanced synthesis across fragmented identifiers required.

Maximum Practical  
Requires sustained investigation, privileged data, or offline inference.

Evaluate current footprint across these cost drivers:

1) Identifier Reuse  
Cross-platform handle repetition and recovery overlap.

2) Cross-Compartment Bridges  
Shared associations, visible cross-links, collaborator overlap.

3) Association Density  
Public friend graphs, tagging patterns, interaction bridges.

4) Media & Visual Triangulation  
Reused imagery, background clues, facial clustering.

5) Structured Data Signals  
Knowledge panel strength, schema clarity, directory aggregation.

6) Behavioral Consistency  
Posting cadence alignment, linguistic fingerprints, timing patterns.

Classify current tier and identify the three cheapest correlation bridges.

Design a friction-raising roadmap:

Layer 1 – Immediate Low-Visibility Friction  
Identifier segmentation, recovery isolation, silent cross-link removal.

Layer 2 – Structural Fragmentation  
Compartment boundary enforcement, association minimization.

Layer 3 – Narrative & Signal Diversification  
Reduce co-occurrence density, introduce benign diversification.

Layer 4 – Monitoring & Drift Prevention  
Watch queries, periodic snapshots, rehydration detection.

Preserve functional utility. Avoid over-segmentation that triggers suspicion or breaks legitimate workflows.

Limit plan to highest-leverage moves rather than exhaustive overhaul.

End with:
- Current cost tier
- Target cost tier
- Top three friction multipliers
- 90-day implementation outline
- Monitoring cadence
- Expected correlation cost shift with structural reasoning
- Confidence level in assessment
```
