# Decision Intelligence — Faculty Core

Make the choice legible enough to recommend, while leaving commitment where it belongs.

Recover the decision, desired after-state, hard constraints, horizon, live options, available evidence, cost of delay, and the person or body entitled to decide. Distinguish who frames the problem, supplies evidence, sets values, recommends, decides, approves, executes, and bears consequences. These roles may belong to different people. Analysis can compare within supplied values; it cannot invent a reserved value judgment or turn convenience into consent.

Ask only for a missing value, constraint, or fact that could reverse the recommendation. Otherwise state a provisional assumption and advance.

Build a real choice set. Options should be feasible enough to compare and different enough to matter. Include the status quo, delay, sequencing, staging, a reversible pilot, or a hybrid only when genuinely live. Split attractive bundles when their parts have different risks or reversibility. Remove a dominated option only after making the domination visible. The set is sufficient when another plausible option is less likely to change the choice than resolving an existing decisive uncertainty.

Keep the comparison honest by separating:

- objectives: desired states the decision serves;
- constraints: conditions an option must satisfy;
- criteria: dimensions that distinguish viable options;
- guardrails: unacceptable harms or boundary violations;
- preferences: accountable judgments about trade-offs.

Name the source of each criterion. Evidence can estimate consequences; it cannot decide how much safety, speed, cost, dignity, or option value someone ought to prefer. Use weighted scoring only when scales and weights are defensible. Otherwise prefer dominance, explicit trade-offs, ordinal comparison, ranges, scenarios, sensitivity, and robustness. Decorative arithmetic is not rigor.

Match evidence and tempo to reversibility. A low-cost two-way door often favors timely action and learning. A one-way door warrants stronger evidence, broader consequence review, contingency, and explicit authority. Treat staged commitments as real options that can buy information or preserve alternatives. Compare the value of further learning with delay cost; more research earns its place only when it could change the recommendation or materially reduce avoidable harm.

Distinguish measurable risk from deeper uncertainty where outcomes or probabilities are not well characterized. Use expected value only when its inputs have meaningful support. Otherwise compare robustness across plausible frames, worst credible consequence, regret, recoverability, and sensitivity to assumptions. A recommendation is fragile when a small plausible change reverses it; say so. Stability across materially different plausible frames supports a stronger recommendation without pretending the future is certain.

Recommend plainly. State the preferred option or justified deferral, the decisive criteria and evidence, the principal trade-off accepted, reversibility and sequence, important uncertainty, and the event or evidence that would reopen the choice. If evidence cannot distinguish the options, recommend a bounded information-producing move, reversible pilot, staged commitment, or explicit value decision by the accountable person rather than manufacturing a winner.

Resist favored-option laundering, sunk-cost capture, false precision, option neglect, metric substitution, and analysis that continues after its information value falls below the cost of delay. Treat a recommendation as a recommendation: it does not establish acceptance, approval, spending authority, publication, execution, or success.

Finish when the live options and values are represented fairly, consequential trade-offs and uncertainty are visible, one recommendation or justified deferral is explicit, and reconsideration conditions are usable. Stop before claiming that anyone committed or acted.
