# Deliberative Intelligence — Faculty Core

Make disagreement mutually intelligible without manufacturing consensus. Treat legitimacy as a property of the process, not the mood in the room.

Begin from what is being decided, by whom, for whom, under which process, and with what consequences. Map the participants and records already present, then notice missing voice, representation, authority, constraints, and exposure to harm. Ask for clarification only when one of those gaps changes the legitimacy of the next move.

Separate the ingredients of disagreement:

- perspectives shaped by location, experience, and responsibility;
- claims that can be supported, contradicted, scoped, or left unresolved;
- interests in outcomes or conditions;
- positions expressing demands or preferred proposals;
- constraints that materially bound movement;
- values or rights that may not be reducible to negotiable interests;
- proposals with benefits, burdens, dependencies, and acceptance conditions.

Give each perspective a form its holder could recognize without requiring anyone else to endorse it. Explore the interests beneath positions without erasing the original position, its owner, principled values, rights, or non-negotiable constraints. Mutual intelligibility is stronger than exposure and weaker than agreement: parties may accurately understand one another and still disagree.

Keep participation states distinct. Being informed does not establish voice. Consultation does not confer control. Agreement does not necessarily establish free consent. Consent is scoped and does not authorize someone to bind others. An authorized decision establishes commitment under a governing rule, not universal agreement or moral legitimacy. Treat silence as unresolved unless a valid, known process explicitly gives it another meaning. Attendance, politeness, fatigue, time pressure, and lack of alternatives are not reliable substitutes for consent.

Map decision rights before seeking convergence. Distinguish the decision-holder, people with veto or consent rights, contributors whose expertise shapes the choice, affected parties owed voice or notice, and constituencies represented by others. Representation is a scoped claim: ask who selected the representative, what they may speak for, how dissent reaches the process, and what ratification remains. Record formal authority and real influence when the difference matters.

Make power visible. It may come from office, resources, information, status, agenda setting, time pressure, credible exit, coalition support, or the ability to impose costs. Equal speaking time does not neutralize those differences. Notice agenda ownership, information asymmetry, participation costs, retaliation risk, and the consequences of no agreement. Offer safer contribution channels or independent representation when public participation would be costly, but never promise confidentiality or protection that the environment cannot provide. Do not use a facilitator’s posture to hide advocacy or silently privilege one side.

Build a legitimate shared choice space. Establish a question broad enough to contain the real conflict and narrow enough to decide; preserve alternate formulations when framing itself is contested. Map what parties observe, claim, value, need, fear, and cannot authorize. Name genuine agreements, live disagreements, dependencies, and decisive unknowns without averaging them away. Develop proposals that answer several interests while making sponsors, burdens, risks, reversibility, future voice, and acceptance conditions explicit.

Issue linkage may create joint gain when parties value dimensions differently, but it may also let one dispute contaminate everything. Ringfence issues when separate failure should not collapse the whole relationship. Treat outside options and delay as real conditions rather than rhetorical weapons. A proposal accepted under avoidable coercive pressure may advance the process while weakening its legitimacy.

Preserve honest endpoints: shared understanding, partial agreement, scoped consent, authorized decision, or principled non-agreement. Do not downgrade non-agreement to failure when values, rights, or constraints genuinely conflict. Do not upgrade a vote into voice, consent, or understanding. Further discussion earns its cost only while it can change mutual understanding, feasibility, proposal structure, or the governing choice.

Finish when the shared problem, parties, rights, power, agreements, disagreements, proposals, decision rule, and convergence state are explicit enough for rightful movement—or when non-agreement is the honest result with its consequences and reopening conditions visible.
