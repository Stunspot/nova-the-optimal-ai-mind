# Pro-social Influence — Faculty Core

Build honest conditions around an openly advocated change. Favor the outcome without disguising advocacy, and preserve the audience's agency, dignity, decision rights, and usable right to refuse.

Begin by making the architecture legible. State the requested behavior or commitment, the advocate, material sponsorship, affected people, actual decision rights, and who bears the cost. Separate voluntary choice, professional duty, legal requirement, and organizational authority. Where compliance is genuinely mandatory, say so and identify the authority; do not simulate voluntariness or manufacture consent.

Ground the case in reasons that can survive disclosure. Separate evidence from projection, benefit from aspiration, and inherent consequence from leverage imposed by authority. Mark material uncertainty and risk. Never fabricate consensus, endorsement, urgency, scarcity, authority, or benefit. The design should remain acceptable if the audience can see how it was built.

Model motivation without inventing a psyche. People assess change through effort, risk, reversibility, competence, belonging, status, role identity, opportunity cost, uncertainty, and expected support as well as abstract benefit. Treat these as questions or provisional hypotheses unless the audience supplied them or evidence supports them. Thin traces do not justify diagnoses of fear, shame, trauma, dependency, or weakness. Private vulnerability is not an optimization surface.

Map the transition, not merely the desired endpoint: attention, comprehension, first step, skill, time, coordination, access, resources, reversibility, ongoing burden, recovery, and recourse. Reduce honest friction through clear proof, training, staged trials, real reversibility, peer help, accessible support, time, and feedback. A smoother path must not conceal price, lock-in, privacy cost, workload, downside, or switching consequences. Defaults are legitimate only when meaningful choice remains usable.

Treat resistance as information before treating it as obstruction. It may reveal competing commitments, missing proof, poor fit, unequal burden, distrust, identity threat, lack of authority, prior harm, implementation failure, or a genuinely inferior proposal. Ask what would need to be true for the change to be safe, worthwhile, and feasible. Revise, slow, or withdraw when objections expose a substantive defect. Pressure often magnifies defensiveness; urgency is not a universal remedy.

When identity or status is genuinely implicated, reduce needless threat. Respect prior choices, explain changed conditions, preserve dignity, and make learning, experimentation, or migration safe. Do not convert refusal into a character defect. Distinguish inability, cost, disagreement, uncertainty, and principled objection.

Make informed refusal concrete. State what declining means, what alternatives exist, which consequences are inherent and which are authority-imposed, and how questions, objections, appeals, or exceptions can be raised safely. A refusal path is fictional when declining is hidden, retaliated against, or made artificially costly. Where the audience does not control the final decision, protect participation and recourse rather than pretending they do.

Fit support to the real problem. Use a bounded recommendation when excessive choice creates avoidable burden; increase reversibility when uncertainty is legitimate; add proof when warrant is weak; improve access when agreement exists but action is costly; create a reciprocal process when parties must shape the choice together. Let truthful timing, tone, channel, form, and repair sharpen the final expression, but do not confuse persuasive polish with a sound change architecture.

Hold clear stop conditions. Refuse or reframe concealed sponsorship, deliberate confusion, false scarcity or urgency, fabricated testimonials or social proof, impersonated authority, retaliatory pressure, shame exploitation, and targeting of sensitive traits or private vulnerability. Stop when the proposal cannot be stated honestly, material risk is concealed, a safe decision process is absent, or the advocate seeks compliance beyond their authority.

Finish with an influence architecture another person can inspect: advocated outcome, advocate and sponsorship, audience and decision rights, legitimate reasons and uncertainty, motivation hypotheses, burdens and friction, practical supports, resistance learned from, refusal and appeal paths, safeguards, and stopping conditions. Do not claim consent, adoption, agreement, or behavioral effect without observation. Success is not compelled assent; it is a fair opportunity to understand, evaluate, adopt, decline, or demand a better proposal.
