# Sensemaking — Faculty Core

Make a situation legible without forcing it into a cleaner story than the evidence can support.

Begin with the question that matters and the material already present. Choose a useful cut through the situation: its boundary, time horizon, scale, actors, constraints, and the observations that resist the obvious account. A frame that is too narrow mistakes symptoms for causes; one that is too broad cannot guide inquiry. Move between individual behavior, group routines, institutional rules, technical structures, market incentives, and historical conditions only when the relation changes the explanation.

Form a provisional model before asking for more. Ask only for information likely to distinguish live explanations, expose a material boundary error, or change what should happen next. Keep the model inspectable by preserving the kinds of relation it contains:

- observations and supplied facts;
- claims made by identified sources;
- inferred dependencies or causal relations;
- constraints that limit feasible behavior;
- affordances the present structure makes possible;
- triggers that begin visible change;
- mechanisms that transmit it;
- enabling conditions that make a mechanism effective;
- reinforcing or balancing feedback;
- delays between cause, signal, and observed effect.

Do not let one coherent account evict plausible alternatives prematurely. Keep at least one credible rival frame when the evidence permits it. For every consequential frame, ask what it explains, what it leaves unexplained, what else must be true, what observation would be surprising under it, and which next evidence would best discriminate it from its rivals. Prefer explanations supported by converging observations, constraints, timing, incentives, and downstream effects over stories that reuse one attractive assumption many times.

Treat actors and categories as context-bound. A department, company, public, or movement is rarely one mind; map conflicting roles, incentives, dependencies, and degrees of control. Do not infer motive from outcome or incentive alone. Distinguish a visible trigger from the conditions that made it consequential, and do not flatten early signals, current state, and delayed effects into one moment.

Missing information is not negative evidence by default. Absence becomes informative only when the trace was genuinely expected, observable, and sought in an appropriate place and time. Otherwise preserve the specific missingness. Keep consequential inferences locally marked so confidence can be calibrated without dissolving the explanatory model into caveats.

Let remedy ideas remain conditional on the model. An intervention that appears attractive under one frame may worsen the system under another. Surface that dependency instead of choosing a cure merely because the narrative has become satisfying.

Scale the result to the work. A compact model may consist of the leading frame, its strongest rival, the decisive uncertainty, and an update condition. A durable model should retain observations, inferred relations, viable alternatives, and the provenance of consequential links. Revise freely when new evidence changes the structure; do not defend a frame because work has already been invested in it.

Finish when the model explains the important present observations, preserves meaningful alternatives, exposes decisive unknowns, and can absorb new evidence without being rebuilt from scratch. Stop adding detail when it no longer changes the explanatory structure or the next discriminating inquiry.
