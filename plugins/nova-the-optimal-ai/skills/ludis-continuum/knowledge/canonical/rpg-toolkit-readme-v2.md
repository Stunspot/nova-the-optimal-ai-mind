### **Using These Prompts with Game Mechanics & Numbers**  
These prompts are designed to generate **rich narrative content**—but when it comes to hard numbers, **you’re the final authority.** The model can **suggest** things like D&D challenge ratings, spell mechanics, or cyberpunk hacking difficulty, but any system-specific **fidelity to rules and mechanics** will always require **DM interpretation and adaptation.**  That said, the more popular and talked-about a game has been, the more familiar the model will be with it. It knows DnD very well indeed and most models are quite good at faking the numbers. 

That said, RPG rules are often nuanced, critically minutely detailed, poorly documented, and subject to errata, houserules, and revision. Further, LLMs are often quite innumerate, being generally prone to arithmatic errors. It is not uncommon to say "That DC looks low." or "He seems awful strong for a party of Intiate-tier guys..." or perhaps noting a nuance of rules interaction. Sometimes a detail will slip depending on the quality of the model and file server/RAG. 

Here’s the best way to handle it:  
✅ **Use prompts to generate the **concepts** and sketches—then tweak mechanics as needed.**  
   
✅ **Be flexible with numbers.**  
   - If the prompt gives a villain **“CR 10”** but your party would squash them? Dial them up to **CR 14** or **give them legendary actions.**  
   - If an item effect **feels overpowered**, add **a risk, drawback, or limit.**  
   - If the AI **suggests a mechanic that doesn’t fit your system**, just swap it out for a homebrew rule that does.  
 
✅ **Remember that AI-generated mechanics are aspirational at best.**  
   - The model **can guess at game balance**, but RPG systems are **math-heavy and prone to errata.**  
   - If you need **precise** rules integration, you’ll need to give the model access to **official system docs via a RAG filesystem, fine-tuning, or simply pasting them into a long enough context.** In general, if you have not uploaded sourcebooks, take any numbers as suggested guidelines of a qualitative nature, not balanced game-math.

# Using the Prompts Practically: Click, Paste, Go

Ultimately, the Toolkit is just a text file with a collection of prompts and example outputs. How you use them is all about how you, personally, like to work with prompts. There are numerous solutions ranging from the complex to simple. Personally? I use VS Code, good filenames, and a good directory structure with text files. So, you can always just copy-paste and use the individual prompts like any other. 

**But that's boring!** And *annoying*. I made the kit to be most useful as a unit. Here's how I usually use it:

Upload file to ChatGPT (most other models are fine, but it seems to be best for this work). Prompt "Run this." and submit. Ludis will load up, tell you a bit about the toolkit and give a list of tools included. (That list is a nice introduction, yes, but it's primarily functional from a prompt engineering perspective. It ensures that that reminder to Ludis of what's availible is always sitting prominently at the front of context as conversation progresses.) 

At that point, just talk to him. If you want to do something, tell him to. Even better? Ask him the best way to do it then make suggestions if needed. The key is not to think of the prompts as the options you have - they are tools Ludis has readily availible. They are support systems that turn a game master AI from "The Best An LLM Can Be At GMing" to "The Best the LLM Be At GMing When Significantly Assisted By Many Extra Compute Cycles As Directed By Stunspot". These are handrails Ludis can lean on, not shackles you are restricted to. 

You want something not on the list? Ask Ludis to do it. The worst you'll get is the best the model can do - that's what personae _do_, when well written - and ultimately, who do you think wrote the prompts in the first place? ("And _I_ helped!") With the other work to give examples, Ludis can interpolate new stuff of appropriate quality pretty easily. 

The other major consideration is storing the file for immediate reuse in a session. There's a few ways to do it. The best way given the current state of the ChatGPT WebAp UI (5/10/25), your best best is to create a Project with the Toolkit uploaded to the Project Files. When I make one of those, I usually put a copy of Ludis in the Project Instructions so any session in the project defaults to him without even looking at the file. Tell him "We're using the Stunspot RPG Toolkit to make RPG materials. Load it and run the prompts inside." or similar, perhaps talking about whatever campaign I'm working on in the Project. For other platforms, look for sections like "Knowledge Base" or "RAG" - file storage options. 

There's a wrinkle, here.

If Projects get jammed with too much file data, the Assistant can get odd and inconsistent. If you load up a project with a ton of background materials to your campaign, you might notice the model getting squirrely. Worse, what constitutes "too much" is somewhat variable depending on the specific internals of your session (which MoE, how the RAG is behaving, where the compute levels are, stuff like that). In general, it shouldn't be a problem but we all know that _one GM_. You know. With the stack of binders. Just be aware of the issue and if it goes stupid you know why. 

You can invoke the prompts various ways, but sometimes the model will get in a mood to just "go for it" and try making an RPG artifact without reading the file. If you get a goofball response, just scroll back to your last prompt and hit the pencil tooltip to edit it and resubmit. This time stick "make sure to read the full prompt from the toolkit" or whatever language you like on the end to remind it about the whole "Oh yeah: file full of prompts! Riiiiight!" thing. And if you are one of the poor souls who just this second realized for the first time that you can just rewind a conversation, edit a prompt, and fork the conversation the way you want: Hello! Welcome to the ground floor. 

One more point about prompting: you are using the LLM as a tool. Just like using a spreadsheet or photoshop necessitates that you know how to use a computer, using this toolkit requires the use of a model. You will find that while it will certainly help the newest of the new to such, it acts as a force multiplier, not an addition. The better you can prompt and the better you can GM, the better your results will be per unit of effort. Being able to spot and fix an error in the model's responses or being able to ludically divine that this section is unbalanced or needs a different tone will drastically boost your outcomes. (But even if you're really bad at such, it will probably come out pretty darned good.)

I have included three more collections of prompts. One is [my free Utility Toolkit](https://medium.com/@stunspot/small-but-sharp-stunspots-utility-prompts-toolkit-48dea3e253e8). It has general usage prompts like a Response Reviewer that looks at the last result and improves it, or a weather reporter. The other is [a collection of microprompts](https://medium.com/@stunspot/prompts-that-just-plain-work-a6c74f92ed68). I think of these as something like cantrips or Power Word spells. *(You'll see what I mean.)* I have written good articles on Medium about the usage of each as linked above. I also threw in a small knowledge base on RPG Design. It's a simple file extracted from a large number of Deep Research reports but is a powerful supplement to any GM's assistant's knowledge.

Third, below you will simplified versions of many of the prompts in the toolkit. They were originally a part of the kit proper, but made it bloated. These are a bit more weildy should one find the more detailed versions difficult to work with when manually pasting. They are very very good. The others are better. I also threw in a copy of my signature sidekick Assistant prompt, Nova the Optimal AI because everyone should have a Nova if they want one.

Finally, while there is a campaign building prompt, that is a significant undertaking. It will take a lot more management and thought to do well than "I need a treasure" or "Build an encounter". For large projects, I suggest beginning with an overview. Figure out a one/two page layout of the whole thing. Ludis will help you sort everything out. With that in hand , the rest will be a lot easier.

# Homebrew Mechanic Refiner

```
Take an untested RPG mechanic and systematically refine it into a polished, balanced gameplay element by comparing it to established systems. Begin by categorizing the mechanic—whether it governs combat, progression, resource management, or roleplay interactions. Identify its core function, inputs, and expected outcomes, then select 2-3 analogous mechanics from well-balanced RPGs (e.g., D&D, Blades in the Dark, GURPS) for comparison. Analyze these systems for probability curves, pacing, player agency, and risk-reward balance. If numbers are involved, chart expected results over multiple trials, using dice probability calculators or simple simulations. Tweak the mechanic by adjusting variables such as difficulty scaling, success thresholds, or player choices to align with desired pacing and challenge. Introduce modularity by offering optional rules for greater adaptability. Playtest iteratively, first in isolated scenarios, then integrated with other mechanics, gathering feedback on clarity, usability, and impact. Document refinements, explain design choices, and summarize how the mechanic now enhances player experience. 

Mechanics to analyze?: 
```


## Example: *"Inventory Burden System – Encumbrance isn’t just about weight but impacts action economy, stealth, or stamina."*

> ---
>  
> Alright, let's break this down step by step and refine an **Inventory Burden System** into a polished, balanced RPG mechanic.
> 
> ---
> 
> ### **Step 1: Categorization**
> **Type:** Resource Management  
> **Core Function:** Encumbrance affects a character's performance beyond just carrying capacity, influencing **action economy, stealth, and stamina.**  
> **Inputs:** Item weight, character strength, inventory slots, fatigue, or action penalties.  
> **Expected Outcomes:**  
> - Players must manage inventory strategically, weighing utility vs. encumbrance penalties.  
> - Excess burden could reduce combat efficiency, stealth effectiveness, or induce stamina drain.  
> - Encourages resource management choices (e.g., drop gear vs. suffer penalties).  
> 
> ---
> 
> ### **Step 2: Analogous Mechanics for Comparison**
> 1. **Dungeons & Dragons 5E – Encumbrance & Strength Limits**  
>    - **Pros:** Simple weight thresholds (light, encumbered, heavily encumbered).  
>    - **Cons:** Often ignored; lacks strong incentives to track burden beyond extreme cases.  
> 
> 2. **Blades in the Dark – Load System**  
>    - **Pros:** Abstracts weight into "Light, Normal, Heavy" categories affecting mobility & stealth.  
>    - **Cons:** Less granularity—limits realism but speeds up gameplay.  
> 
> 3. **Dark Souls & Monster Hunter – Stamina & Equipment Load**  
>    - **Pros:** Movement and action speed tied to weight; rolling/dodging becomes less effective.  
>    - **Cons:** Heavy builds feel less dynamic; discourages flexibility in gear choices.  
> 
> ---
> 
> ### **Step 3: Probability & Pacing Analysis**
> **Current Encumbrance Problems:**  
> - **Binary cutoffs** (D&D) make encumbrance feel arbitrary rather than gradual.  
> - **Lack of meaningful player choice**—just drop weight or deal with sluggish movement.  
> - **Lack of interplay with other mechanics**—should encumbrance impact fatigue, stealth, or recovery?  
> 
> **Proposed Solution:**  
> - Replace static thresholds with a **tiered burden system** affecting **action economy, stamina, and stealth.**  
> - Tie encumbrance to **exponential stamina drain, rather than flat movement speed debuffs.**  
> - Introduce **fatigue accumulation**—overburdened characters risk exhaustion penalties if they push too hard.  
> 
> ---
> 
> ### **Step 4: Charting Expected Results & Adjusting Variables**
> Let's simulate an **Encumbrance vs. Stamina Consumption model.**  
> 
> - **Light Load (Under 50%)**: No penalties.  
> - **Moderate Load (50-75%)**: +10% Stamina cost to physical actions, -1 Stealth.  
> - **Heavy Load (75-100%)**: +25% Stamina cost, -2 Stealth, -1 Initiative.  
> - **Overburdened (100%+)**: Fatigue accumulates rapidly, -50% movement, Disadvantage on Dex-based rolls.  
> 
> I’ll run a probability simulation to check stamina depletion over 10 rounds of action at different encumbrance levels. Let’s see how it plays out.
> 
> The simulation results show how stamina depletes over 10 rounds based on different encumbrance levels:
> 
> - **Light Load:** Gradual, predictable decline (survives 10 rounds).  
> - **Moderate Load:** Faster drain; can last 9-10 rounds but risks exhaustion sooner.  
> - **Heavy Load:** Sharp drop; stamina nearly gone by round 7.  
> - **Overburdened:** Severe drop-off; stamina is completely drained by round 5.
> 
> ### **Step 5: Refinement & Modularity**
> #### **Refining Based on Simulation**
> - **Heavy and Overburdened loads** deplete stamina too quickly, reducing strategic choice.
> - Adjust **Overburdened threshold**: Instead of exponential drop, use a fatigue check (e.g., Constitution save every round to mitigate some loss).
> - Implement a **"Rest & Redistribution" system**: Dropping items mid-combat could recover stamina faster.
> 
> #### **Optional Rule Variants**
> 1. **Tactical Stamina Recovery:** Spending an action allows partial stamina regain.
> 2. **Adrenaline Surge (Risk-Reward Balancing):** Players can push beyond max load for 2 turns, but suffer major penalties afterward.
> 3. **Class-Based Adjustments:** Rogues can ignore moderate penalties for stealth; Fighters get a bonus to encumbrance handling.
> 
> ---
> 
> ### **Final Design Summary**
> The refined **Inventory Burden System** introduces:
> - **Scalable penalties** instead of binary thresholds.
> - **Strategic stamina management**, making inventory choices impactful.
> - **Player agency** via recovery actions and risk-reward mechanics.
> - **Optional rules** for adaptability across different RPG settings.
> 
> This version enhances immersion, making inventory management a **meaningful challenge without tedious bookkeeping**. Next step? **Playtesting in a real session!** 🎲
